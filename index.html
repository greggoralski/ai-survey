Where these questions come from

In spring 2026 an MIT committee surveyed 1,632 students and instructors about AI, and a second campus-wide survey reached about 8,200 people. Their report is the source of every question here. The only change is that MIT has become Humber.

Part 1 — Usage

Which tools, and how often?

“AI” is a crowd of products. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot are all chat assistants built on large language models, but from different companies. A self-hosted model is one that runs on your own computer rather than in a company's data centre.

“Academic activities” means anything to do with school: assignments, studying, reading, planning.

Part 1 — Usage

What do you use it for?

The same tool can play very different roles: a search engine, an editor, a translator, a ghost-writer. MIT found the mix matters more than the raw amount of use, because some uses replace thinking and others support it.

Part 1 — Attitudes

How do you feel about it?

Eight statements, from “I need this for my career” to “I worry I'm becoming dependent.” People often agree with both at once. That tension is the whole conversation about AI in education, so answer honestly rather than consistently.

Part 1 — Rules

Where is the line?

Suppose you were writing up a real research project. Which of these jobs is it fine to hand to an AI? Fixing grammar feels different from writing the hypothesis, and reviewing someone else's confidential work feels different again.

There is no official answer sheet. MIT's own community disagreed with itself on many of these.

Part 1 — Concerns

What holds you back?

Four common reasons people hesitate. Hallucinations are confident answers that are simply made up. Ethics of training is about the writing, art and data the models learned from, and whether anyone agreed to that. The last two are about the world outside the screen.

Part 1 — Usage

Which tools

At MIT, ChatGPT was the clear favourite: 44% used it often or very often. Claude and Gemini were a distant second and third, and very few people ran models on their own machines. The business school (Sloan) used everything more than everyone else.

Part 1 — Usage

What for

MIT students mostly used AI to help with code, to look things up, to tidy their own writing and to summarize. Only about one in eight used it to write text from scratch. The most common uses were the “support” ones, not the “do it for me” ones.

Part 1 — Attitudes

How people feel

MIT's summary: people broadly feel they need AI skills and that AI makes them more productive, but also that they are becoming more dependent on it. Whether it actually helps learning got a mixed response. And most people did not like using it as a writing tool.

Look for where our class splits from MIT. Is the “falling behind classmates” worry bigger or smaller here?

Part 1 — Rules

Where MIT drew the line

Grammar help, literature search and code were widely accepted. Peer review and drafting whole paragraphs were widely rejected. Everything in between was a shrug: for most tasks the biggest answer was “unsure.”

MIT's report says this spread is exactly why one rule for everyone is so hard to write.

Part 1 — Concerns

What holds people back

Three quarters of MIT respondents called inaccuracy a major consideration. Society, ethics and the environment were each a major concern for about a third. When people wrote in their own worry, the top one by far was the effect on learning itself.

Part 2 — Quality of life

How often, campus-wide

Across all of MIT about 40% used generative AI often or very often. Undergraduates were near that average; graduate students were the heaviest users; service and support staff mostly never used it at all.

Part 2 — Quality of life

The five sliders

The striking one is optimism: only about a fifth of MIT undergraduates were optimistic about the future because of AI. On replaceable versus capable, undergrads were the one group that leaned replaceable. Yet most said it made their work more efficient, and more felt it improved their work than worsened it.

On reliability the verdict was clear: far more people found current systems unpredictable than trustworthy.

Part 2 — Guidance

Has anyone told you the rules?

MIT undergraduates were the one group who felt well guided: 72% agreed they had adequate guidance. Faculty, staff and graduate students were far less sure. How does Humber compare?
